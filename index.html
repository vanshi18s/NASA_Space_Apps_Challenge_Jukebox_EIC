Earth Jukebox

NASA’s Earth data was made to be seen. Choose a dataset and hear how our planet is changing.

How the sound is made

Latitude → pitch
12 bands from 70°N to 50°S in an A minor pentatonic scale. North is high, south is low.
Light → loudness
Denser, brighter lights are louder, on one fixed scale for every year. Gray land is ignored. On the map, the glow beside the gold bar shows the loudness of each latitude band.
Bright hubs
The brightest clusters ring like a bell when the scan reaches them and stay marked on the map. A brighter hub rings louder, and its pitch follows latitude.
Longitude → time and stereo
The scan moves west to east and the sound moves from the left ear to the right.
Compare mode
Shows 2012 above 2023 and scans 2012 first, then 2023. One soft blip marks 2012 and two blips mark 2023.
Year label
The year printed in NASA’s video is never analysed. It sits outside the map area we read, and that spot is also masked.

Space play or stop · H home · I current settings. Sliders move in steps with a rising tick, and a low thud marks each end.

Earth Jukebox is an independent project made for the NASA International Space Apps Challenge. NASA does not endorse this project or any non-U.S. Government entity and is not responsible for the content of non-U.S. Government websites.

Imagery comes from NASA’s Scientific Visualization Studio (SVS), including “Change in Night Lights between 2012 and 2023 – EIC Version” (SVS 5313), based on NASA Black Marble data from Suomi NPP VIIRS. We turn NASA’s published visualizations into sound. The frames are display-stretched pictures, not calibrated measurements, so the sound shows patterns and direction of change, not exact values. Choices such as the musical scale and instrument tones are artistic; what is loud, high or rising comes from the data. Do not use it for scientific analysis or safety decisions.
